The 7 SaaS KPIs Every Founder Must Track Before Their Series A

A practical guide to the metrics investors will interrogate — and how to build a dashboard that makes them impossible to hide from.

The honest truth most founders don't want to hear: Most early-stage SaaS founders are flying blind. They check Stripe daily, glance at their user count, and call that "data-driven." Then they walk into a Series A meeting and a VC asks: "What's your net revenue retention trend over the last 6 months?" Silence. That silence is expensive.

This guide covers the 7 KPIs that investors will probe in every serious fundraising conversation — what they mean, how to calculate them, what healthy looks like, and most importantly, how to build a live dashboard that tracks them automatically so you're never caught off-guard.

You don't need a data team. You don't need a six-figure BI platform. You need the right metrics, tracked consistently, visualized clearly.

Why most SaaS founders track the wrong things

The mistake isn't ignoring data — it's tracking vanity metrics that feel good but tell investors nothing about the health of the business. Total signups, page views, app downloads — these numbers are easy to grow and meaningless in isolation.

The one question your metrics must answer: "If we give you $2 million, do you have enough evidence that each dollar spent acquiring a customer will return more than a dollar in lifetime value — and do you have the operational efficiency to prove it will keep compounding?"

The 7 KPIs below are the specific answers to that question. Master these and you walk into any investor room ready.

1. Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR)

MRR is the heartbeat of your SaaS business. It is the single most important top-line number because unlike one-time revenue, it compounds. A business growing MRR at 15% month-over-month doubles roughly every 5 months.

How to calculate it

MRR = Number of paying customers × Average Revenue Per Account (ARPA) per month

If you have 50 customers paying $99/month and 10 paying $299/month: MRR = (50 × $99) + (10 × $299) = $4,950 + $2,990 = $7,940

What investors look for

  • Consistent month-over-month growth rate — not the absolute number
  • The composition of MRR: new MRR vs expansion MRR vs churned MRR
  • Seasonality patterns and how you explain them

📊 Dashboard tip from MA TechHub

Track New MRR, Expansion MRR, Churned MRR, and Net MRR separately in your dashboard. A waterfall chart showing these components makes your growth story visually compelling in any investor deck.

2. Customer Churn Rate

Churn is the silent killer of SaaS businesses. A 5% monthly churn rate means you're replacing your entire customer base every 20 months. You can grow all you want — if the bucket is leaking, you're running in place.

How to calculate it

Monthly Churn Rate = (Customers lost in month ÷ Customers at start of month) × 100

If you started July with 200 customers and lost 8: Churn = (8 ÷ 200) × 100 = 4%

Benchmarks by stage

  • Early stage (<$1M ARR): up to 5–7% monthly is survivable if you're learning fast
  • Growth stage ($1M–$10M ARR): target below 2% monthly
  • Series A and beyond: investors expect sub-1.5% monthly or you need a compelling explanation

3. Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)

CAC tells you the price tag of one new customer. On its own, the number is meaningless. In relation to LTV, it becomes the most important ratio in your business model.

How to calculate it

CAC = Total Sales & Marketing spend in a period ÷ Number of new customers acquired in that period

If you spent $10,000 on marketing in Q1 and acquired 40 customers: CAC = $10,000 ÷ 40 = $250

The number that matters most: CAC Payback Period

CAC Payback Period = CAC ÷ (ARPA × Gross Margin %)

A payback period under 12 months is strong. Under 6 months is exceptional. Over 18 months will raise eyebrows at any serious Series A pitch.

4. Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)

LTV answers: "On average, how much revenue will one customer generate before they leave?" Combined with CAC, it defines whether your business model is fundamentally sound.

How to calculate it

LTV = ARPA ÷ Monthly Churn Rate

If ARPA = $150/month and monthly churn = 2%: LTV = $150 ÷ 0.02 = $7,500

The LTV:CAC ratio

This single ratio summarises your entire unit economics story:

  • Below 1:1 — you are destroying value with every customer you acquire
  • 1:1 to 3:1 — marginal; you need to improve before raising
  • 3:1 — the gold standard investors want to see
  • Above 5:1 — excellent, but may signal you're underinvesting in growth

5. Net Revenue Retention (NRR)

NRR is arguably the most powerful metric in this list — and the one most early-stage founders have never even heard of. It measures whether the revenue you already have is growing or shrinking, independent of new customer acquisition.

How to calculate it

NRR = [(Starting MRR + Expansion MRR − Churned MRR − Contraction MRR) ÷ Starting MRR] × 100

An NRR above 100% means your existing customers are spending more over time — your business grows even if you acquire zero new customers. This is the definition of a defensible SaaS business.

Why investors obsess over NRR

  • NRR > 120% is considered best-in-class (Slack, Snowflake territory)
  • NRR > 100% means your growth is compounding without needing to constantly refill a leaky bucket
  • NRR < 80% is a hard flag in any Series A conversation

📊 Dashboard tip from MA TechHub

Build a cohort retention table in your dashboard — it shows NRR broken down by the month each customer joined. This gives investors confidence that your retention is consistent, not a one-time spike.

6. Burn Rate & Runway

Your burn rate is how much cash you consume per month. Your runway is how many months until you hit zero. These two numbers determine your timeline for everything: when to hire, when to pivot, when to raise.

How to calculate them

Gross Burn Rate = Total monthly operating expenses

Net Burn Rate = Monthly expenses − Monthly revenue

Runway = Cash in bank ÷ Net Burn Rate

What healthy looks like

  • 12 months of runway minimum before starting a Series A process
  • 18–24 months is ideal — gives you negotiating leverage
  • Below 6 months: you're raising from a position of desperation, not strength

7. Activation Rate

The most undertracked metric on this list. Activation rate measures the percentage of new users who reach your product's core value moment within a defined timeframe — usually 7 or 14 days.

This matters because an unactivated user is not really a user. They signed up and left before experiencing what makes your product valuable. High signups with low activation is a product problem masquerading as a growth problem.

How to define and calculate it

First, define your activation event — the specific action that signals a user has experienced core value. Examples:

  • A project management tool: user creates and assigns their first task
  • An analytics platform: user connects a data source and views their first dashboard
  • A fintech app: user completes their first transaction

Activation Rate = (Users who completed activation event within 7 days ÷ Total new signups) × 100

Target: above 60% within 7 days. Below 30% and your onboarding needs urgent attention before you invest more in acquisition.

Your Series A metrics checklist

Before your next investor conversation, make sure you can answer every one of these questions without hesitation:

  1. What is our current MRR and what was it 3, 6, and 12 months ago?
  2. What is our monthly customer churn rate and what is driving it?
  3. What is our blended CAC and our CAC payback period by channel?
  4. What is our LTV:CAC ratio and how has it trended over the last 6 months?
  5. What is our Net Revenue Retention and what does our cohort table show?
  6. What is our current net burn rate and how many months of runway do we have?
  7. What is our activation rate and what is our defined core value moment?

The bottom line

Investors don't fund potential — they fund evidence. These 7 KPIs are the evidence they need. Founders who walk into Series A conversations with a live dashboard showing all 7 metrics trending positively close rounds faster, at better valuations, with less dilution.
